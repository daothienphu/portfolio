## How this happened

So recently I've been working on a game set entirely in an alien aquatic environment. Think Dave the Diver + Dredge, so it's got that exploration, adventure, and eldritch horror in it.

The game is still in limbo hell right now, because instead of making the actual game, I keep building the world, which has never been tested through gameplay, and can be changed anytime...

So anyway, here's another piece of world building lore I made for this world: a conlang. I felt like an exploration game of this nature should have a language of the native species, whether extinct or not, to expand the world. The player can then find bits and pieces of the language to translate relics, conversations,... and all that makes the world less empty. (Editor's note from the future: Subnautica 2 did it, yay)

Now to give it a little identity, and to save me the trouble of having to repeat information: the species is called Kashat (which means shoal in the conlang, so effectively the species is called The Shoal).

The player doesn't meet a Kashat. There is no friendly native speaker to point at a fish and say its name. Everything the player learns about the language has to come from old records, like shells or coral fragments. Another thing about the world is that in the modern day, the world, or at least the part of the world that the player interacts with, has 2 distinct seasons, the Hot and the Cold. Those pieces of info gave several constraints that shaped a lot of how the language came to be:

- The language had to be real enough to be decoded.
- It needed regular rules a player could notice, test and get wrong.
- A vocabulary built from a consistent system rather than a pile of invented words.
- A writing system that gives honest clues about pronunciation.
- The language must have 2 variations, one for the Hot and one for the Cold.

And that's how my conlang was born: **Ghesen** and **Ghoson** (the Hot's version and the Cold's version of the name).

This post is basically a dump of everything the language currently is, why each piece exists, and how it hooks back into the world. I tried to keep it readable for game designers and world builders, but there's enough phonetics and grammar in here that linguists can poke holes in it too (please do, nicely). I linked references as I went and collected them all at the bottom. And at the very end there's a word list and some practice sentences, if you want to try reading Kashat yourself.

## First, the world

Before any of the language stuff makes sense, you need to know what the Kashat were dealing with, because honestly the seasons are doing like 80% of the design work here.

In the **Hot**, the upper ocean blooms. Open water is full of food, and full of things that want to eat you. Down below, the volcanic vents wake up and erupt. So life in the Hot is: feast, roam, flee fire.

In the **Cold**, ice seals the surface into a roof. The vents die, and the seabed goes barren. Life in the Cold is: huddle, ration, wait.

From that, I set 4 rules for myself that the whole language has to follow:

- **Season is grammar.** Every word can be said in a Hot way or a Cold way, and the difference actually means something.
- **Vertical is everything.** When you live in open water, left and right don't matter nearly as much as up toward the surface and down toward the vents. So surface and depth replace left and right.
- **Every fact names its sense.** It's dark down there half the year. How you know something matters as much as what you know.
- **The body shapes the sound.** The Kashat have no lips, but they do have organs humans don't, and those organs make noise.

Everything below is just me following those 4 rules until they turned into actual systems.

## What it sounds like

### Four noise-makers

Kashat speech comes from 4 places:

- the **throat**, which makes guttural consonants humans can roughly make
- the **swim bladder**, which makes drummed pulses
- the **pharyngeal teeth** (bony grinding plates in the throat), which make clicks and rasps
- the **gill covers**, which make a fluttering buzz

Fun fact, the swim bladder and the throat teeth thing aren't me making stuff up. A lot of real fish actually talk like this. Drums and croakers (yes, that's what they're called, family Sciaenidae) and toadfish vibrate their swim bladders with special sonic muscles to make thumps and knocks, and other fish click by grinding bony bits together, which is called stridulation. If you want the actual biology, the [DOSITS page on how fish make sounds](https://dosits.org/animals/sound-production/how-do-fish-produce-sounds) is a really easy read. Having real fish to base this on helped a lot, because it gave me a believable "range" for each sound: low, felt thumps from the bladder, sharper higher clicks from bone.

And since there are no lips, the language has no lip sounds at all. No *p*, *b*, *f*, *v*, *w*, or lip-made *m*. That's on purpose, and it's one of those things a player will notice pretty quickly once they start writing stuff down. It's an early "oh, this definitely isn't a human language" moment.

### Throat consonants

These are written in lowercase. Most of them are sounds English doesn't really use, but other human languages do. That was deliberate: if you want something to sound back-of-the-throat and alien, the easiest way is to steal real sounds that are made really far back in the mouth. Here they are:

- **q**: a *k* made way further back, against the uvula (the dangly bit at the back of your mouth). Arabic has this one, it's the letter *qaf*.
- **kh**: the sound at the end of Scottish *loch*.
- **gh**: a gargled sound, close to a French *r*.
- **h**: a harsh, breathy *h* squeezed out from deep in the throat. Arabic's "hard h", the letter *haa*.
- **c**: a strangled squeeze from deep in the throat. Arabic's letter *ayn*.
- **'**: the glottal stop, the little catch in the middle of *uh-oh*.
- **t, d, k, g, s, sh, z, l, n, ng, y**: roughly like English.

If you've never heard some of these, the [interactive IPA chart](https://www.ipachart.com/) has a recording for each one (and the exact phonetic symbols, if you're a linguist and want them). Wikipedia also has decent explainers on [uvular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uvular_consonant) and [pharyngeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharyngeal_consonant) consonants if you want to know where in your throat they happen.

### The not-human sounds

These 4 come from organs we don't have, so I write them in uppercase to make them stick out:

- **M** (swim bladder): a deep thump, felt more than heard. Closest human version: thump your chest, or do a low hum.
- **K** (pharyngeal teeth): a sharp, bony snap. A tongue click works.
- **R** (pharyngeal teeth): a long grinding rasp. Think "krrrr" through your teeth.
- **X** (gill covers): a fluttering buzz. The closest human version is a lip trill, which is kind of funny for a species with no lips.

My favorite detail in the whole sound system: the gills are physically separate from the throat. So **X can sound over a vowel**. A Kashat can literally say two things at once, a vowel from the throat and a buzz from the gills at the same time. Linguists would call this a kind of co-articulation, except with two completely independent sound sources, which human mouths can only sort of fake. In-world, poets abuse this a lot.

### Vowels and syllables

There are 6 vowels, and they come in pairs. Each Hot vowel has one fixed Cold partner:

- **i** (Hot) pairs with **u** (Cold)
- **e** (Hot) pairs with **o** (Cold)
- **a** (Hot) pairs with **aw** (Cold), said like the "aw" in *saw*

Quick note on that last one: **aw** is written with two letters, but it's one single vowel. And since the language has no *w* sound at all (no lips, remember), "aw" can never be confused with anything else. Free spelling, courtesy of fish anatomy.

Syllables are (C)V(C), so: maybe a consonant, a vowel, maybe a consonant. The exception is the 4 non-human sounds. Those can go anywhere, and can even be a whole syllable by themselves, like *M* or *KK*.

## The Hot/Cold flip (the main mechanic)

### Roots are skeletons

This is the part I'm proudest of, so bear with me.

Every root in the language is just a skeleton of consonants. I borrowed this idea from Semitic languages like Arabic and Hebrew, where a [triconsonantal root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_root) like K-T-B carries a core meaning ("writing"), and putting different vowels inside it gives you related words (book, writer, wrote, and so on).

In Ghesen/Ghoson, the vowels have one job: they set the season. **Put Hot vowels in a root and you get its active, abundant, outward meaning. Put Cold vowels in and you get its enduring, stored, waiting meaning.** Nouns and verbs share the same roots, and you tell them apart by where they sit in the sentence.

Some examples:

- **Q-L-N**: *qilen* (Hot) is the bloom, surface food. *qulon* (Cold) is stored fat, reserves.
- **T-H-K**: *tehek* (Hot) is vent-fire, an eruption. *tohok* (Cold) is a dead vent, cold stone.
- **N-GH-L**: *nighel* (Hot) is open water, to swim free. *nughol* (Cold) is the ice-roof, to be trapped under it.
- **K-SH-T**: *kashat* (Hot) is the traveling shoal, to school. *kawshawt* (Cold) is the winter huddle, to huddle.
- **GH-S-N**: *ghesen* (Hot) is to sing, to call. *ghoson* (Cold) is to recite, to remember.

That last one is where the language's name comes from. In the Hot it's *Ghesen*, "the calling". In the Cold it's *Ghoson*, "the remembering".

The important thing is that the 2 forms are almost never just "same word, different time of year". They split into related but different meanings, and the split always follows the same logic. Hot is what's happening and plentiful. Cold is what's kept, waited out, or survived. Feasting becomes fasting. Warmth and desire become loyalty and shared body heat. The predator of the Hot becomes hunger, which is the predator of the Cold. I love that one.

### For the linguists

Phonetically, the flip is basically "push every vowel back and round it". Hot *i* and *e* are front and unrounded, their Cold partners *u* and *o* are back and rounded, and the low *a* moves back and rounds into *aw*. So a Cold word is, feature-wise, its Hot word with all the vowels backed and rounded.

That makes it a cousin of front/back [vowel harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel_harmony) in languages like Finnish and Turkish, mixed with the internal vowel change linguists call [apophony or ablaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apophony) (same thing that gives English *sing / sang / sung*). The difference is that here, one single binary choice, Hot or Cold, controls every vowel in the word at once.

### Harmony rules

3 rules spread the season beyond the root itself:

- **Suffixes match the word they attach to.** *qilen-ti* ("from the bloom") but *qulon-tu* ("from the reserves").
- **Pronouns and numbers match the main verb.** They "follow the water". So "I" is *sek* in a Hot sentence and *sok* in a Cold one.
- **Switching a whole sentence into Cold vowels makes it solemn or formal**, even in the middle of summer. So the language gets a formal register for free, and the world gets an idiom out of it: "speak in Cold vowels" means "be serious".

Plurals aren't spoken, they're drummed: *sek-M* is "we few", *sek-MM* is "we, the whole shoal".

### Words with no season

A few words have no vowels at all, which means they can't take a season. They belong to neither, and they're sacred or taboo:

- **MKXM**: the Turning, the passage between seasons
- **RM**: death, "the grind, then silence"
- **XM**: zero, nothing, "the felt silence"

What I like here is that the grammar itself explains why these words are special. The 2 biggest transitions in a Kashat's life, the season changing and dying, literally sit outside the season system.

## Up, down, past, future

### Depth cases

Because the Kashat think vertically, location is marked by 4 case suffixes that describe where something is in the water column. Each one has a Hot and Cold form:

- **Surfaceward**, *-ish / -ush*: above, up toward. Also used for "more than", "better than", and "hope".
- **Depthward**, *-ek / -ok*: beneath, down at. Also used for "less than", "origin", and "ancestry".
- **Level**, *-an / -awn*: alongside, with. Also "together" and "equal to".
- **Source**, *-ti / -tu*: from, out of. Also "because of" and "made of".

So *sok-M-ush* is "above us", *nughol-ok* is "beneath the ice", and *qilen-ti* is "from the bloom".

The extended meanings are where the world leaks in. Up means hope, because the surface is where the bloom comes back. Down means origin and ancestry, because that's where the old, settled things are.

### Vertical tense

The same suffixes also work as tense on verbs. **The past sinks and the future rises.**

- *nisek-ek* (depthward): "it woke"
- *nisek* (nothing): "it wakes"
- *nisek-ish* (surfaceward): "it will wake"

Ancestors are "the deep ones", and the unborn are "the rising ones". Using the same suffixes for space and time isn't that weird actually, human languages do it constantly ("the years ahead", "put that behind us"). The Kashat just picked a different axis.

## Evidentials: how do you know that?

### What evidentiality is

[Evidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidentiality) is a grammar feature where you have to mark where your information came from. Did you see it? Hear it? Get told about it? A bunch of real languages actually require this. Tariana and Tuyuca in the Amazon are the famous ones, with 5-way systems. If you want to go deep, Alexandra Aikhenvald's book [Evidentiality](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/evidentiality-9780199204335) (Oxford, 2004) is the go-to, and the World Atlas of Language Structures has two good chapters, one on [what evidentials distinguish](https://wals.info/chapter/77) and one on [how they're marked](https://wals.info/chapter/78).

### The Kashat version

In dark water, how sure you are depends on which sense you used. So every statement of fact in Ghesen/Ghoson gets a suffix naming its source, ranked from most to least trusted:

- **-X**, felt, through the lateral line and pressure waves. Treated as absolute. Lying with *-X* is a serious crime.
- **-li / -lu**, seen, by glow. High trust.
- **-K**, heard. Medium trust.
- **-sa / -saw**, tasted, from a chemical trace in the water. Basically "something passed through here".
- **-ngi / -ngu**, told by someone else. Gossip.
- **nothing**, the speaker did it themselves, so it's self-evident.

The top rank is based on real fish again. The [lateral line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_line) is the sense fish use to feel movement and pressure in the water around them. For a species that spends half the year in the dark, it makes sense that this is the sense they trust the most.

Also, the felt marker is the gill-flutter X itself, and it's the one marker you can't whisper. So you can't quietly fake it. Commands, questions, and future stuff about other people usually skip the evidential entirely. A future event marked with *-X* is a prophecy, and only elders are allowed to say those.

## Grammar

Word order is **Verb, Subject, Object**. The action goes first, because in an emergency what matters is *flee* or *eat*, not who's doing it. VSO is uncommon but real in human languages too, Welsh, Irish, and Classical Arabic all do it.

The verb template:

```
(R- negative) (K- imperative) ROOT -tense -evidential
```

The noun template:

```
ROOT (-M / -MM / -MMM or a numeral) -case
```

Two examples of the verb template doing its thing:

- *R-di'en-ek-ngi tin* = NEG-feast-PAST-HEARSAY they, "They say she didn't eat."
- *K-kawshawt sok-MM* = IMP-huddle we-SHOAL, "Everyone, huddle!"

And a few more rules:

- **Drum plurals.** *-M* is a few, *-MM* is many or the whole group, *-MMM* is uncountable, "as the bloom".
- **Alarm fronting.** One drum before a noun yanks it to the front as an alert. *M tehek!* is "Vent-fire!"
- **Questions** end with a double click, *KK*.
- **Register.** Slow, spaced drumming is formal and solemn ("Cold-voiced"). Fast drumming is casual. Slow drums plus Cold vowels is the voice of funerals, laws, and the Turning rites.

## Counting in sixes

### Why 6

The Kashat count in base 6. Each side of the body has 3 gill slits, so a "full gill" of 6 is the natural bundle, the same way our 10 fingers gave us base 10. Base 6 isn't just a sci-fi thing either, the Ndom language in Papua New Guinea is the usual real example of a [senary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senary) system.

Numbers also take the season of the sentence. Hot numbers count what you gained. Cold numbers ration what's left.

### Digits and big numbers

The digits:

- 0: *XM* (no season, "the felt silence")
- 1: *tik / tuk*
- 2: *shen / shon*
- 3: *gha'a / ghaw'aw*
- 4: *lisk / lusk*
- 5: *nehi / nohu*

And the powers of 6, which reuse roots that already mean something:

- 6: *khal / khawl*, "a full gill"
- 36: *kash / kawsh*, "a shoal"
- 216: *qilen / qulon*, "a bloom / a full store"
- 1,296: *nighel / nughol*, "open water / the ice-roof"

### Building numbers

Numbers go biggest to smallest. A multiplier goes in front of its power with a hyphen, and each part is joined to the next with a single drum, *M*, which means "and".

Take 100. In base 6 that's 244, because:

$$
100 = 2 \cdot 6^2 + 4 \cdot 6^1 + 4 \cdot 6^0
$$

So 100 in Hot form is *shen-kash M lisk-khal M lisk*: "two-shoal and four-gill and four". A smaller one: 15 is $2 \cdot 6 + 3$, so *shen-khal M gha'a*.

Some other number rules:

- A number goes after its noun and replaces the drum plural. *qilen gha'a* is three blooms, never *qilen-M gha'a*.
- **Ordinals** use the surfaceward case, because rank is height. *tik-ish* is first, *shen-ish* is second.
- **Fractions** are "part from whole", using the source case. *tik shen-ti* is "one from two", so a half.
- A season is split into 6 tides, and a full cycle of one Hot and one Cold is *MKXM shen*, "two Turnings".
- **Age is only counted in Colds survived.** "How old are you?" is literally "How many Colds have you endured?" Hots don't count, anyone survives a Hot.

## Writing: the grown scripts

### Three scripts, three kinds of permanent

The Kashat don't write with ink. They grow, carve, or wear their writing. Over their history they ended up with 3 scripts, and each one trades speed for how long it lasts:

- **Zeket** is knots tied into living kelp. Fastest, least permanent. It's for anything that was never meant to be kept: messages, tallies, warnings.
- **Sidek** is scratched or pressed into a Kashat's own shell right after molting, before it hardens. A fresh molt takes a while to harden, then a while more to fall off, so a sidek mark lives on your body for a season or two. Because it's carved into your own body, you can't deny it or fake it, so it's reserved for stuff that has to be sincere: tattoos, tribal marks, courtship tokens, confessions. A kept molt-piece, a *sudok*, is one of the most intimate gifts a Kashat can give.
- **Zokot** is coral grown over a carved stone frame. It takes many cycles to make and outlives whoever wrote it. It's for laws, lineages, oaths, and the records of the Turnings. Writing a lie in zokot is taboo, because the lie will outlast you.

Before the Kashat figured out how to work shell and coral, kelp was all they had. You can still see that in the vocabulary: *zeket* comes from the root Z-K-T, "to knot", and "tie it in kelp" still means "it's temporary, don't count on it". The coral version, "grow it in coral", means swear it, make it permanent. And sidek got its own: "marked in the molt" is an oath or confession you can't take back.

### Glyphs that tell you how to pronounce them

All 3 scripts share one design, and this is the part that matters most for the game. The script is [featural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Featural_writing_system), meaning the shape of a glyph tells you how the sound is made. The famous human example is Korean [Hangul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul), where the consonant letters were originally designed to show the shape of your mouth and tongue when you say them. Tolkien's Tengwar works on a similar idea.

Here's how the Kashat version works:

- **Each word is one upright stalk.** In kelp it's a strand, in coral it's a branch.
- **Branches are consonants.** Throat sounds branch to the left, mouth sounds branch to the right. How many branches and what shape tells you the exact consonant. On the left, 1 short spur is *'*, 2 is *h*, 3 is *c*, and 1, 2, or 3 long branches are *q*, *kh*, and *gh*. On the right, wavy branches are *s / sh / z* and hooked branches are *l / n / ng*.
- **Which way the branches point is the season.** In a Hot word, every branch reaches up. In a Cold word, every branch curls down. Season is written once for the whole word, which matches how the vowel harmony works when spoken.
- **Branch tips are the vowel.** A point is *i / u*, a fork is *e / o*, a bulb is *a / aw*. Combine that with the direction and you get the exact vowel.
- **Non-human sounds are grown onto the stalk itself.** M is a ring of polyps around the stalk (stacked for MM and MMM). K is a notch cut in. R is a serrated ridge. X is a sea-fan crown.

Text grows **bottom to top**, past to future, depth to surface, so you read the way coral grows. A sentence is laid out like a coral colony: the verb is the main trunk with its evidential sitting on top as a crown, and the subject and object grow off the sides. Since a felt-evidential X crown is a big sea fan, you can see it from far away, which is why coral laws are crowned with fans.

Numbers are a single stalk cut into segments. The bottom segment is the units, and each segment up is worth 6 times more. Each segment holds 0 to 5 notches, and a smooth empty segment is zero. So 100 (base 6: 244) is a 3-segment stalk with 4 notches at the bottom, 4 in the middle, and 2 at the top.

### Why this matters for the game

This solves a problem I didn't realize I had until later: if nobody's alive to speak the language, how does the player ever learn what it sounds like? The writing does it. Once a player gets the branch anatomy, they can sound out a word they've never heard, just from how it was grown. And that's roughly how real linguists figure out how dead languages were pronounced, from the logic of the spelling, not from recordings.

## Turning it into a game

### What survived

The 3 scripts don't survive equally, and that unevenness is basically the backbone of the discovery system:

- **Zokot is everywhere.** Coral is literally designed to outlive the people who made it. It's the main source, the closest thing the player gets to a textbook. It's also stiff and formal: laws, lineages, rites, mostly in the solemn register.
- **Sidek only survives by accident.** Brine pockets, burial niches, anywhere luck kept a shed molt from crumbling. It's rare, broken, and personal. It's where names, family, love, and confession live, all the stuff formal law never bothers writing down.
- **Zeket has almost completely rotted away**, except for one sealed cave system cut off from the open ocean, where the kelp never fully decayed. Since kelp was only ever used for casual everyday writing, this cave has the register nothing else has: messages, warnings, tallies, gossip.

So what the player knows ends up shaped by the physics of the world. They'll understand Kashat law way before they understand how Kashat actually talked to each other, and the parts of the language that were never meant to last are exactly the parts that are hardest to find. Which I think is kind of sad, in a good way.

### Learning from old records

The goal is that the player figures out the rules from evidence instead of reading them in a tutorial pop-up. The best real-world example of this is the decipherment of [Linear B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B). Before anyone knew what language it was even writing, Alice Kober noticed some words showed up in sets that only differed in their endings, and figured out the language had inflection. Michael Ventris later built on her work and showed it was an early form of Greek. Structure first, meaning after.

Ghesen/Ghoson is built so the same kind of noticing is possible. A Hot and a Cold inscription of the same root only differ in their vowels when spoken, and only in branch direction when written. Case suffixes repeat across tons of nouns. Drum plurals show up as stacked rings. All patterns a careful player can spot before they know a single word.

And games have already shown this loop works. inkle's [Heaven's Vault](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-inkle-developed-its-own-ancient-language-for-i-heaven-s-vault-i-) built a whole story around translating an invented ancient script, where wrong guesses become part of the story and get corrected later. Rundisc's [Chants of Sennaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chants_of_Sennaar) has you decode multiple invented languages by watching people and filling in a notebook of guesses. The difference with mine is the setting: there's nobody to talk to, so every clue is archaeological.

### Practice sentences

Try translating each one before you peek at the answer.

*Yilegh-li qilen-MM. Di'en-ish sek-MM.*

Answer: rise-SEEN bloom-MANY, feast-FUTURE we-SHOAL. "The bloom is rising (I see its glow). We will feast."

*M tehek! Nisek-X tehek.*

Answer: ALARM vent-fire, wake-FELT vent-fire. "Fire! The vent is waking. I feel it."

*Nughol-X sok-M-ush. K-kawshawt sok-MM, tohok-ok.*

Answer: be-roofed-FELT we-FEW-SURFACEWARD, IMPERATIVE-huddle we-SHOAL, cold-stone-DEPTHWARD. "The ice is closing over us. Huddle, all of us, down at the dead stone."

*Du'on-ok hawl noq KK?*

Answer: endure-PAST you how-many QUESTION. "How many Colds have you endured?", which is how a Kashat asks "How old are you?"

*Qilen-ti qulon.*

Answer: bloom-SOURCE reserves. "From the bloom, the store." A proverb, basically "gather while you can".

*Du'on-ush hawl KK? Du'on-ush. Qilen-ish.*

Answer: "Will you endure?" "I will endure. Toward the bloom." This is the goodbye the Kashat say to each other at the Turning, as the ice starts forming.

That last one is my favorite line in the whole language so far. It uses almost everything in this post at once: the Cold root for enduring, the future rising toward the surface, the double-click question, and the Hot root for the bloom, pulling the whole sentence back toward hope at the end. If a player can read that line without help by the end of the game, then the language did its job.
